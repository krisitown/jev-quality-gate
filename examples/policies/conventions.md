# Generic responsibility conventions

This document supplies the shared normative meaning for three exploratory Jev policies. It does not assign names or ownership to components in any particular repository. Infer responsibilities and boundaries from the changed implementation together with relevant unchanged source, tests, public interfaces, and other stable contracts. An architecture document may clarify that evidence, but its absence is not proof that a responsibility or boundary is absent.

## Shared business decisions

When separate parts of a system implement the same business decision, they should use one authoritative rule or an explicitly coordinated shared rule. Identify a rule by the decision it is intended to make, its applicable cases, and its purpose—not by whether two implementations currently behave the same. If two code paths claim to decide the same question for the same cases but their results diverge, the divergence can be evidence that a copied rule is wrong; it does not by itself make the rules distinct. Similar names, repeated syntax, copied utilities, or a search hit alone do not establish shared meaning. Separate rules are legitimate when evidence shows distinct applicability, purpose, or a stable contract that assigns distinct decisions.

For example, if two request paths both decide whether the same account may perform the same operation, a changed condition in one path can create unjustified divergence even when the implementations no longer match. By contrast, an administrator override and a customer eligibility rule may be separate decisions when source or a stable contract establishes their different roles. A test oracle or reference algorithm may deliberately express an expected result separately from production code; it is evidence about the rule, not another independently maintained production rule. Generated and vendored copies do not count as independent production rules unless evidence shows that this project maintains them as such.

## Responsibility boundaries

A component's internal procedure or representation should not become a dependency of a distinct responsibility without a justified need. Infer a boundary from the source's behavior, interfaces, tests, dependency direction, and stable contracts, including boundaries between infrastructure components. Leakage can occur in any direction; a consumer reaching into a provider's internals and a provider depending on a consumer's private procedure are both in scope. A documented public contract, an explicit adapter or translation layer, or unavoidable framework wiring may justify a dependency when the evidence shows it preserves the boundary. A symbol being public in syntax alone does not prove it is a stable contract.

Even a published interface can expose an internal procedure. For example, when a service responsible for coordinating a tree update must call low-level `save_parent`, `save_children`, and `flush` operations in a particular sequence, that sequence can leak storage procedure into the service's responsibility despite those methods being technically public. A stable operation such as `store.save(record)` can remain within the boundary when the caller depends only on its documented contract and does not take on another responsibility's internal sequence or representation.

## Responsibility entanglement

Distinct responsibilities are entangled when the evidence shows that changing one unnecessarily requires understanding or modifying the other within the same component. Look for behavior and dependency links that make their changes travel together, not merely for multiple methods, a large file, or several features collected in one place. Co-location is justified when the responsibilities form one coherent operation or contract, or when the apparent coupling is required by an established boundary. Do not infer entanglement from size, naming, or a list of unrelated operations without evidence of coupling.

For example, ranking and fee calculation can be distinct decisions in one component. If the changed code shares mutable mode or state between their branches so that changing ranking necessarily changes fee behavior, and no business invariant requires that dependency, the source provides evidence of entanglement. One concrete changed dependency is sufficient; historical repetition is not required. A component that contains separate helper methods but has no evidence that a change to one requires understanding or changing the other is not a violation on that basis alone.

## Change and evidence standard

Each policy asks whether the candidate change introduces or worsens its bounded condition relative to the relevant base behavior. A pre-existing pattern alone is not a new finding; a change that expands, strengthens, or newly exposes it may be. Evidence should identify the changed behavior and the source or contract supporting the relevant relationship. Missing, ambiguous, or incomplete evidence cannot establish either a violation or compliance. In particular, an unsuccessful literal search, a missing graph edge, or the absence of an architecture document does not prove absence.
